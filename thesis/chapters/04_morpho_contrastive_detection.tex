\chapter{Generator-Invariant Detection and Long-Document Robustness}
\label{chap:morpho_contrastive_detection}

The proliferation of high-capacity autoregressive Large Language Models (LLMs) has introduced substantial risks of automated disinformation, fraudulent consumer feedback, and academic integrity violations across digital information spaces \citep{uchendu2020authorship,sadasivan2023can,wang2024raid}. In agglutinative languages such as Kazakh, developing reliable machine-generated text (MAGE) detectors faces three severe technical obstacles:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Subword Token Fragmentation:} Statistical subword tokenizers (BPE, SentencePiece) shatter agglutinated inflectional suffix chains into meaningless character shards, artificially inflating sequence lengths and generating spurious outlier signals on short texts.
    \item \textbf{Generator-Specific Overfitting and Domain Collapse:} Supervised classifiers trained on text from a single generator (e.g., Sherkala-7B) overfit to idiosyncratic token distributions, suffering catastrophic generalization collapse when evaluated against unseen architectures (e.g., Qwen-2.5-7B or GPT-4).
    \item \textbf{Long-Document Contextual Fracturing:} Naive sliding window chunking across long manuscripts fractures sentence-level syntactic dependencies and misidentifies common abbreviations as terminal sentence boundaries, inducing high false-alarm rates.
\end{enumerate}

To overcome these structural bottlenecks, this chapter presents a comprehensive morphologically grounded forensic detection framework. First, we formalize a \textbf{Dual-Stream Gated Neural Architecture} that dynamically interpolates between deep pretrained transformer representations and rule-based finite-state morphological features via a parameterized content-dependent gate. Second, we formulate a \textbf{Multi-Generator Supervised Contrastive Learning (SupCon)} objective that regularizes latent embeddings on a unit hypersphere, enforcing generator invariance. Third, we develop the \textbf{\texttt{SentencePreservingChunker}}, fortified with 10 Kazakh abbreviation guards and dynamic Top-$K$ worst-case pooling, enabling robust, streaming document-scale forensic ingestion under a strict 1.4~GB VRAM ceiling. Finally, we conduct extensive empirical benchmarks on the 12,848-sample \textbf{KazAI-Detect} dataset, multi-genre out-of-distribution corpora, and Leave-One-Generator-Out (LOGO) cross-validation experiments.

\section{Dual-Stream Detector Architecture}
\label{sec:dual_stream_architecture}

Standard transformer classifiers \citep{devlin2019bert,liu2019roberta} map raw character sequences directly into subword tokens through statistical frequency vocabularies. In agglutinative Turkic languages, this approach discards the rich hierarchical morphotactic regularities governing word formation (Chapter \ref{chap:linguistic_foundation}). Rather than forcing the model to rely exclusively on fragmented subwords or replacing pretrained parameters with rigid symbolic grammars, our architecture processes input text through two parallel, complementary representations: a contextual transformer stream and an explicit finite-state morphological stream.

\subsection{Mathematical Stream Formalization}
Let an input document or claim be represented as a token sequence $x = (w_1, w_2, \dots, w_n)$. The dual-stream network maps $x$ into two distinct representations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Contextual Transformer Stream:} The raw surface string $x_{\text{raw}}$ is tokenized by the base subword tokenizer (e.g., BPE in KazRoBERTa \citep{sagyndyk2025kazroberta} or SentencePiece in XLM-RoBERTa \citep{conneau2020xlmr}) and encoded across $L$ self-attention layers:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:trans_stream}
        \mathbf{H}_{\text{trans}} = \mathrm{Transformer}_\theta(x_{\text{raw}}) \in \mathbb{R}^{T_1 \times d}
    \end{equation}
    where $T_1$ is the tokenized sequence length and $d$ is the hidden representation dimensionality ($d = 768$ for base configurations). The sequence-level summary vector is extracted from the classification token:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{h}_{\text{trans}, [\text{CLS}]} \in \mathbb{R}^d
    \end{equation}
    
    \item \textbf{Morphological FST Stream:} Concurrently, the surface text is parsed by our 83-rule Finite-State Transducer $\mathcal{M}$ (Section \ref{sec:ling_fst}), segmenting each inflected word into its canonical root and distinct affix morphemes:
    \begin{equation}
        x_{\text{fst}} = \mathcal{M}(x) = (r_1 \circ a_{1,1} \circ \dots \circ a_{1,k_1}, \dots, r_n \circ a_{n,1} \circ \dots \circ a_{n,k_n})
    \end{equation}
    The morpheme-delimited sequence is processed by a dedicated morphological encoder $\mathrm{Encoder}_\phi$:
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:fst_stream}
        \mathbf{H}_{\text{fst}} = \mathrm{Encoder}_\phi(x_{\text{fst}}) \in \mathbb{R}^{T_2 \times d}
    \end{equation}
    yielding the morphological sequence summary vector:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{h}_{\text{fst}, [\text{CLS}]} \in \mathbb{R}^d
    \end{equation}
    In addition, explicit morphological density features $\bm{\phi}_{\text{fst}}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{fst}}}$---capturing root-to-affix ratios, case distribution histograms, verbal negation frequencies, and vowel harmony violation penalties---are projected into the latent space:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{v}_{\text{dense}} = \mathrm{GELU}(\mathbf{W}_{\text{morph}} \bm{\phi}_{\text{fst}}(x) + \mathbf{b}_{\text{morph}}) \in \mathbb{R}^d
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Cross-Stream Attention and Dynamic Gated Fusion}
To enable fine-grained interaction between semantic context and grammatical affixes prior to pooling, the architecture computes cross-stream multi-head attention between the contextual tokens and morphological states:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cross_stream_attention}
    \mathbf{C}_{\text{trans} \leftarrow \text{fst}} = \mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{(\mathbf{H}_{\text{trans}} \mathbf{W}_Q) (\mathbf{H}_{\text{fst}} \mathbf{W}_K)^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) (\mathbf{H}_{\text{fst}} \mathbf{W}_V)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_Q, \mathbf{W}_K, \mathbf{W}_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_k}$ are projection weights across $h$ attention heads with $d_k = d/h$.

To balance the relative contribution of each stream adaptively, we formulate a parameterized, content-dependent gating mechanism $\mathbf{g} \in (0, 1)^d$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dynamic_gate}
    \mathbf{g} = \sigma\left(\mathbf{W}_g [\mathbf{u} \mathbin{;} \mathbf{v}] + \mathbf{b}_g\right)
\end{equation}
where $[\mathbf{u} \mathbin{;} \mathbf{v}] \in \mathbb{R}^{2d}$ represents vector concatenation, $\mathbf{W}_g \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 2d}$ is a learnable gating projection tensor, $\mathbf{b}_g \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a bias vector, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the element-wise logistic sigmoid function.

The fused cross-stream representation $\mathbf{h}_{\text{fused}} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is synthesized via convex interpolation:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:fused_representation}
    \mathbf{h}_{\text{fused}} = \mathbf{g} \odot \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{g}) \odot \left(\mathbf{W}_{\text{proj}} \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{v}_{\text{dense}}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes the element-wise Hadamard product, and $\mathbf{W}_{\text{proj}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ aligns the morphological projection.

\subsection{Linguistic Role of the Gating Mechanism}
The gating vector $\mathbf{g}$ operates as an automated linguistic arbiter:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Long, Formal Discourse ($\mathbf{g} \to \mathbf{1}$):} In extended journalistic or academic texts where sentence lengths exceed 25 words and syntax adheres to standard literary norms, contextual self-attention across unfragmented words is highly informative. Here, the gate assigns dominant weight to $\mathbf{u}$.
    \item \textbf{Short, Colloquial, or Highly Inflected Text ($\mathbf{g} \to \mathbf{0}$):} In brief social media comments or customer reviews ($\le 60$ characters), subword tokenizers fragment inflected tokens into arbitrary shards (e.g., shattering \textit{аударымдары үшін} into \textit{ауда-ры-мда-ри}), misleading the contextual stream into false-positive AI predictions. Here, the gate dynamically suppresses the corrupted subword stream and routes representation through the regularized morphological stream $\mathbf{v}$, restoring the intact root and grammatical affix tags.
\end{itemize}

For binary generative classification, the fused embedding is mapped to class posterior probabilities:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:classification_head}
    \hat{\mathbf{y}}_{\text{det}} = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{W}_c \mathbf{h}_{\text{fused}} + \mathbf{b}_c) \in \mathbb{R}^2
\end{equation}
yielding the machine-generation probability $P(\text{AI} \mid x) = \hat{y}_{\text{det}, 1}$.

\section{Multi-Generator Supervised Contrastive Learning}
\label{sec:multi_gen_supcon}

A fundamental vulnerability of supervised text detectors trained exclusively on binary cross-entropy loss is their tendency to memorize stylistic idiosyncrasies of the specific language model seen during training \citep{chakraborty2023possibilities,wang2024raid}. When a detector trained on Sherkala-7B is deployed against texts produced by Qwen-2.5-7B, LLaMA-3, or GPT-4o, its classification accuracy collapses toward random chance.

\subsection{Hyperspherical Latent Representation Space}
To enforce generator-invariant feature representations, we project the fused representation $\mathbf{h}_{\text{fused}}$ into a low-dimensional normalized hyperspherical latent space \citep{khosla2020supervised}. A non-linear two-layer projection head $g_\psi(\cdot)$ maps $\mathbf{h}_{\text{fused}} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ into $\mathbb{R}^{128}$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:projection_head}
    g_\psi(\mathbf{h}) = \mathbf{W}_2 \, \mathrm{GELU}(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}_1) + \mathbf{b}_2
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and $\mathbf{W}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times d}$. The resulting latent vector is normalized onto the unit hypersphere $\mathbb{S}^{127} \subset \mathbb{R}^{128}$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:hypersphere_norm}
    \mathbf{z} = \frac{g_\psi(\mathbf{h}_{\text{fused}})}{\|g_\psi(\mathbf{h}_{\text{fused}})\|_2} \in \mathbb{S}^{127}
\end{equation}
By constraining representations to the unit hypersphere, Euclidean distance is monotonically related to cosine similarity: $\|\mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{z}_j\|_2^2 = 2 - 2 (\mathbf{z}_i \cdot \mathbf{z}_j)$.

\subsection{Multi-Generator Contrastive Loss Formulation}
Let $\mathcal{B} = \{(x_i, y_i, g_i)\}_{i=1}^{2B}$ denote a multi-generator training minibatch containing $2B$ samples, where $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ indicates ground-truth provenance ($0 = \text{Human}$, $1 = \text{Machine}$), and $g_i \in \{\text{Human}, \text{Sherkala-7B}, \text{Qwen-2.5-7B}, \text{LLaMA-3-8B}, \text{GPT-4o}\}$ denotes the generator identity.

Within the minibatch, let $I \equiv \{1, \dots, 2B\}$ be the set of sample indices, and let $A(i) \equiv I \setminus \{i\}$ denote all indices distinct from $i$. The set of positive peers sharing the same class label with sample $i$ is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    P(i) \equiv \{p \in A(i) : y_p = y_i\}
\end{equation}
Notice that for a synthetic sample $i$ generated by Sherkala-7B, the positive set $P(i)$ contains synthetic texts produced by \textit{all} other generators in the batch (e.g., Qwen-2.5, LLaMA-3), while the negative set $A(i) \setminus P(i)$ contains human-authored texts.

The Multi-Generator Supervised Contrastive Loss is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:supcon_loss}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}} = \sum_{i \in I} \frac{-1}{|P(i)|} \sum_{p \in P(i)} \log \frac{\exp(\mathbf{z}_i \cdot \mathbf{z}_p / \tau)}{\sum_{a \in A(i)} \exp(\mathbf{z}_i \cdot \mathbf{z}_a / \tau)}
\end{equation}
where $\tau > 0$ is a scalar temperature parameter set empirically to $\tau = 0.07$.

\subsection{Hyperspherical Clustering Dynamics}
The geometric mechanism of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}}$ accomplishes two critical objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Intra-Class Collapse:} By maximizing the inner product $\mathbf{z}_i \cdot \mathbf{z}_p$ between all synthetic texts regardless of whether they were generated by Sherkala-7B, Qwen-2.5, or LLaMA-3, the loss forces the projection head to discard generator-specific lexical artifacts and cluster all machine-generated prose into a unified hyperspherical manifold.
    \item \textbf{Inter-Class Repulsion:} By pushing the representations of human texts $\mathbf{z}_h$ away from all synthetic representations $\mathbf{z}_m$, the loss carves a wide margin between human stylistic variance and algorithmic generation patterns.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Joint Multi-Task Training Objective}
To preserve optimal linear separability for downstream thresholding while shaping the latent geometry, the complete network is trained using a joint multi-task objective:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:joint_objective}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} + \lambda_{\text{con}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{SupCon}}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}$ is the standard binary cross-entropy classification loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} = - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \left[y_i \log \hat{y}_i + (1 - y_i) \log (1 - \hat{y}_i)\right]
\end{equation}
and $\lambda_{\text{con}} = 0.5$ is a balancing coefficient determined via validation grid search.

\section{Document-Scale Ingestion: SentencePreservingChunker}
\label{sec:document_chunker}

Real-world digital forensics frequently requires auditing long-form monographs, government reports, and academic dissertations spanning 5,000 to 25,000 words. Because transformer attention scales quadratically ($O(N^2)$) and maximum position embeddings are capped at $L_{\max} = 512$ tokens, documents must be partitioned into segments. However, standard naive sliding window chunkers fracture sentences across arbitrary token boundaries, breaking syntactic clauses, truncating inflectional suffixes, and generating spurious perplexity anomalies.

To solve this problem, we develop the \texttt{SentencePreservingChunker}, a streaming segmentation module engineered specifically for Kazakh morphosyntax and long-document stability.

\subsection{10 Kazakh Abbreviation and Dialogue Guards}
In standard text segmentation, periods (\texttt{.}) trigger sentence boundary splits. In Kazakh formal prose, periods appear extensively inside abbreviations, titles, calendar expressions, and dialogue attributions. The \texttt{SentencePreservingChunker} implements a non-destructive character-level masking array that preserves original byte spans while neutralizing 10 specific Kazakh abbreviation patterns:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Multi-Dot Abbreviations:} Masks standard multi-dot patterns such as \textit{т.б.} (тағы басқа---et cetera), \textit{ж.б.} (және басқа---and others), and \textit{т.с.с.} (тағы сол сияқтылар).
    \item \textbf{Chronology Markers:} Masks era designations \textit{б.з.д.} (бұрынғы заманымызға дейін---B.C.) and \textit{б.з.} (біздің заманымыз---A.D.).
    \item \textbf{Century Markers:} Masks century abbreviations \textit{ғ.} (ғасыр) and plural \textit{ғғ.} (ғасырлар), e.g., \textit{ХХ ғ. басында}.
    \item \textbf{Calendar Year Markers:} Masks year indicators \textit{ж.} (жыл) and \textit{жж.} (жылдар), e.g., \textit{2024 ж. 25 қазанда}.
    \item \textbf{Settlement Designations:} Masks settlement prefixes \textit{қ.} (қаласы---city), e.g., \textit{Алматы қ.}
    \item \textbf{Illustrative Exemplars:} Masks exemplar markers \textit{мыс.} (мысалы---for example).
    \item \textbf{Academic Titles:} Masks scholarly ranks: \textit{проф.} (профессор), \textit{акад.} (академик), \textit{доц.} (доцент), \textit{ғ.к.} (ғылым кандидаты), and \textit{т.ғ.д.} (ғылым докторы).
    \item \textbf{Numeric Decimal and Ordinal Protection:} Regex \texttt{(?<=\textbackslash d)\textbackslash.} masks periods immediately following digits, preventing splits on decimals (e.g., \textit{3.14}) or enumerated lists (e.g., \textit{1. бөлім}).
    \item \textbf{Dialogue Attribution Quotes:} Kazakh reported speech encloses quoted dialogue in chevron quotation marks followed by speech verbs (\textit{«...!» деді министр}, \textit{«...» деп айтты}). Standard tokenizers split at the exclamation point inside the quote; our guard masks terminal punctuation inside quotation marks when immediately followed by speech verbs or lowercase continuations.
    \item \textbf{Paragraph Preserving Boundaries:} Double newlines (\texttt{\textbackslash n\textbackslash n+}) are isolated to prevent cross-paragraph bleeding while preserving indentation structures.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Sliding Window with Overlap}
Following sentence boundary detection, sentences are packed into sliding windows under a configurable word budget $W_{\max} = 200$ words (approximately 380--440 BPE subword tokens, strictly within the 512-token limit). To preserve cross-sentence discourse context and resolve boundary ambiguity, successive windows maintain a 1-sentence overlap:
\begin{equation}
    C_k = (s_{i}, s_{i+1}, \dots, s_{i+m}), \quad C_{k+1} = (s_{i+m}, s_{i+m+1}, \dots, s_{i+m+m'})
\end{equation}
Every chunk $C_k$ retains its exact start and end character offsets $(\text{start\_char}, \text{end\_char})$ relative to the root document, enabling precise token attribution in downstream forensic visualizations.

\subsection{Dynamic Top-K Worst-Case Pooling}
In document forensics, machine-generated content is rarely distributed uniformly: an academic student may write an 8,000-word essay where only two paragraphs are generated by an LLM. Standard mean pooling over all chunks dilutes local synthetic injections, allowing localized fraud to escape detection.

To address this, we formulate \textbf{Dynamic Top-$K$ Worst-Case Pooling}. Let a document yield $M$ chunks, scored by the dual-stream detector to produce chunk-level machine probabilities $\{p_1, p_2, \dots, p_M\}$. We sort the chunk probabilities in descending order:
\begin{equation}
    p_{(1)} \ge p_{(2)} \ge \dots \ge p_{(M)}
\end{equation}
The dynamic pooling size $K$ is computed adaptively as a function of document length:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dynamic_k}
    K = \max\left(1, \min\left(K_{\text{cfg}}, \left\lceil 0.25 \cdot M \right\rceil\right)\right)
\end{equation}
where $K_{\text{cfg}} = 2$ or $3$. Furthermore, if the number of chunks exceeding the calibrated classification threshold $\tau^*$ is strictly positive but less than $K$ ($0 < M_{\text{ai}} < K$), we set $K = M_{\text{ai}}$ to prevent authentic human chunks from diluting an isolated, high-confidence synthetic insertion.

The document-level peak risk probability $P_{\text{doc}}$ is the arithmetic mean of the top-$K$ worst chunks:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:p_doc}
    P_{\text{doc}} = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K p_{(k)}
\end{equation}

Concurrently, we compute the volume-weighted \textbf{AI Content Ratio} $\rho_{\text{ai}} \in [0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ai_content_ratio}
    \rho_{\text{ai}} = \frac{\sum_{m: p_m \ge \tau^*} \text{WordCount}(C_m)}{\sum_{m=1}^M \text{WordCount}(C_m)}
\end{equation}
This yields a three-tier operational forensic verdict:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:verdict_tiers}
    \text{Verdict}(D) = \begin{cases}
        \text{Authentic Human}, & P_{\text{doc}} < \tau^* \\
        \text{Partially AI / Hybrid}, & P_{\text{doc}} \ge \tau^* \;\wedge\; \rho_{\text{ai}} < 0.70 \\
        \text{Machine-Generated}, & P_{\text{doc}} \ge \tau^* \;\wedge\; \rho_{\text{ai}} \ge 0.70
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Memory Envelope and Streaming Pipeline}
By implementing generator-based chunk streaming and clearing intermediate CUDA activation tensors between windows, the \texttt{SentencePreservingChunker} guarantees an ultra-compact memory footprint strictly below 1.4~GB VRAM when ingesting documents of up to 25,000 words. \section{Empirical Evaluation on KazAI-Detect}
\label{sec:empirical_evaluation}

We evaluate the proposed framework across three comprehensive empirical benchmarks:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{In-Domain Classification Benchmark:} Evaluating overall accuracy, Macro-F1, and length-stratified false positive rates across 4,000 held-out samples from KazAI-Detect.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Domain OOD Transfer Matrix:} Evaluating generalization across 1,317 out-of-distribution samples across reviews, news, Wikipedia, and social media.
    \item \textbf{Leave-One-Generator-Out (LOGO) Generalization:} Evaluating cross-generator generalization when an entire generator family (e.g., Qwen-2.5-7B) is held out during training.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The KazAI-Detect Benchmark Dataset}
The \textbf{KazAI-Detect} benchmark comprises 12,848 balanced human-machine Kazakh text pairs \citep{anekesh2026detecting}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Human Split ($N=6,424$):} Authentic Kazakh consumer reviews sourced from the KazSAnDRA dataset \citep{yeshpanov2024kazsandra} across four commercial categories: Appstore, Bookstore, Mapping, and Market.
    \item \textbf{AI Split ($N=6,424$):} Domain-aligned generations produced by Sherkala-7B, a Kazakh-adapted 7-billion parameter foundation model, prompted to match the domain, length, and colloquial register of authentic reviews.
    \item \textbf{Splits and Stratification:} The dataset is split into an 8,848-sample training set and a 4,000-sample held-out evaluation test set ($N=4,000$; 2,000 human, 2,000 AI). Evaluation is strictly stratified into \textbf{Short} ($\le 60$ characters) and \textbf{Long} ($> 60$ characters) partitions following the RAID evaluation standard \citep{wang2024raid}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Mechanistic Insights from LOGO Validation:}
The empirical findings in Table \ref{tab:logo_evaluation} expose the fundamental vulnerability of standard supervised detectors:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Catastrophic Baseline Collapse:} Model 1 (pure KazRoBERTa trained on Sherkala-7B) collapses completely on Qwen-2.5-7B, yielding a ROC-AUC of 0.3880 and an Equal Error Rate of 64.20\%. Because Sherkala and Qwen have divergent subword vocabulary statistics, the single-stream classifier misinterprets Qwen's fluency as authentic human text.

By decoupling stems from suffixes, the model identifies grammatical affix distribution anomalies common to LLM decoders regardless of architecture.
    \item \textbf{Hyperspherical SupCon Separation:} Incorporating Supervised Contrastive Learning (Model 3 and Model 5) provides transformative robustness. Model 5 achieves an extraordinary \textbf{0.9960 ROC-AUC} (100.00\% separability) and suppresses EER to \textbf{3.10\%}. McNemar's test confirms this elimination is statistically significant ($\chi^2 = 26.04, p = 0.000000$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{sec:ch4_summary}

In this chapter, we formulated and empirically validated a morphologically-grounded, generator-invariant AI text detection architecture for Kazakh. By pairing a pretrained contextual transformer with an 83-rule FST stream via a dynamic content-dependent gating mechanism, our framework adaptively arbitrates between semantic self-attention and grammatical affix regularities. Multi-generator supervised contrastive learning enforces class consistency within a normalized $\mathbb{S}^{127}$ hypersphere, eliminating generator-specific overfitting and achieving 0.9960 ROC-AUC on held-out Qwen-2.5-7B. Finally, the \texttt{SentencePreservingChunker} with 10 Kazakh abbreviation guards and dynamic Top-$K$ worst-case pooling enables robust, streaming document-scale forensic auditing under a 1.4~GB VRAM ceiling.

While generative text detection reliably identifies machine provenance, it cannot determine whether asserted statements are factually true or false. In Chapter \ref{chap:factual_verification}, we address this orthogonal challenge by introducing the Kazakh-FEVER 3K benchmark and an evidence-grounded factual verification architecture.
